How to read the IR statements
  • “Let …” introduces an object, and “Suppose that …” adds an assumption. Both stay in force to the end of the text, or of the bullet they are in.
  • “There is T ∈ ℕ, depending only on …, with the following property.” chooses T, and the rest of the text is the property; when the property is a list, it reads “with the following properties:”. Lean checks the list of dependencies. The list is shown only when the property opens with a new variable (“Let …” or “for every …”), so that the reader sees that T does not depend on it. Otherwise T may depend on everything above, and the list is left out.
  • “All of the following hold:”, “The following are equivalent:” and “At least one of the following holds:” are followed by bullets.
  • A word in italics is the label of a Lean constant, made from its name; point at it to see the constant. A label with one argument reads “X is label”, otherwise label(X, Y, …) with Lean's argument order. Some labels read as a short sentence instead, such as “𝒢 achieves time vector t on 𝒞”; the label's definition in the glossary uses the same sentence. Its negation reads “X is not label …” when the sentence has “is”, and again takes no quantifier after it.
  • “Let K = …” names a term that occurs several times. (x, y) is a pair, [x, y] a list, and () the only element of the one-point type. {0, …, k − 1} is Lean's Fin k. ⟨x⟩ is x as an element of a subtype, such as C(z) in the range of C; Lean supplies the proof of membership. “A member of S” is an element of the set S as a type, and “the set F” is a finite set F used as a set.
  • “Let t ≥ T.”, “for every t ≥ T”, “for every i < n” and “there is i < n such that” range over natural numbers.
  • A proof passed to a label is not shown when Lean can prove it from the assumptions. “label(X, Y) does not hold” and A ⊈ B are negations; no quantifier follows “does not hold”. Many names follow the paper (𝒞, ℋ, t⋆, …), to make reading the two side by side easier; each statement still says what its names stand for.
  • A statement that begins with “Setting A.” begins with the sentences of Setting A, listed under “Throughout”. The checker puts them in place of the mark.
  • A label with a dotted underline links to its definition in the glossary at the end of the page. A definition reads “By definition, … if and only if …”; Lean proves it by Iff.rfl, so it is the label's definition, unfolded once.
  • {x(n) : n < t} is the set of the first t terms of x, and ∖ is set difference. “O has a membership oracle” means that O comes with a correct test for x ∈ Li.

The paper’s setting (2)

  • Let Σ be a finite alphabet set (e.g., {0,1},{a,b,…,z}), and let Σ* denote the set of all strings of finite length formed by concatenating elements from Σ in any order. A language L is simply a countable subset of Σ*, and we will always be concerned with generating from a countable collection 𝒞={L1,L2,…} of languages. For generation in the limit to make sense, we will assume that |Li|=∞ for every i.

    p. 6

  • The setup assumes that a target language K∈𝒞 is chosen and fixed, and thereafter, strings from K are provided sequentially as input in the form of an enumeration x1,x2,x3,…. The choice of K and the order of enumeration can possibly be chosen by an adversary. Repetitions of strings are permitted in the enumeration—the only requirement is that every string in K appears at least once in the enumeration, i.e., for every z∈K, z=xt for some t<∞. We use the shorthand St to denote the set of all distinct strings seen in an enumeration x1,…,xt up until t.

    p. 6

Theorem 1 Every countable collection is nonuniformly generatable

In the paper Theorem 1, p. 3

There exists an algorithm that non-uniformly generates in the limit from every countable collection of languages.

Terms from the paper (1)

  • An algorithm non-uniformly generates in the limit from languages in a collection 𝒞, if for any language K∈𝒞, there exists a t*=t*(𝒞,K) such that for any enumeration of K presented to the algorithm, the string zt generated by the algorithm at time step t belongs to K\St for all t satisfying |St|≥t*.

    p. 7

IR statement checked by Lean

Let α be a type. Assume that α is infinite. Assume that α is countable. Let 𝒞 be a sequence of languages over α.

Suppose that L is set infinite for every L ∈ range(𝒞).

Then 𝒞 is nonuniformly generatable.

Theorem 5 Uniform feedback generation is characterized by finite GF dimension

In the paper Theorem 5, p. 5

A collection 𝒞 of languages can be uniformly generated with feedback if and only if its GF dimension is finite.

Terms from the paper (1)

  • A collection 𝒞 can be uniformly generated from with feedback if there exists a generator strategy G and a constant t*=t*(𝒞), such that for every language K∈𝒞 and for every adversary strategy A consistent with K, in the transcript T(A,G), zt∈K\St for every t satisfying |St|≥t*.

    p. 27

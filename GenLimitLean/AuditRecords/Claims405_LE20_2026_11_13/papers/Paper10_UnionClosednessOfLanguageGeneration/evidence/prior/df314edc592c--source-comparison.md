## Independent full-source inventory (recorded before Stage 1)

**Model and background (pp. 2-3, 6-8).** The universe is countable strings over a finite alphabet, with integer/natural encodings in the constructions. Languages are infinite (notation p. 7, Remark 2.2, p. 10 footnote 5). A deterministic generator is a sequence of arbitrary mappings from length-n input histories to one output string; no computational resource or computability restriction is imposed. The introduction's footnote permits repeated input strings but Remark 2.3 adopts repetition-free enumerations throughout; with repetitions, distinct observed strings must be counted for uniform/nonuniform bounds. Enumerations are sound and complete. Correctness at a round means membership in the target minus the input examples seen so far. Freshness is relative to input history, not prior outputs. Definition 2.1 adds an empty-residual escape clause, immaterial for infinite targets and finite histories. The quantifier patterns are: limit generation `exists G, forall K, forall E, exists n*, forall n>=n*`; uniform `exists G, exists n*, forall K, forall E, forall n>=n*`; nonuniform `exists G, forall K, exists n*(K), forall E, forall n>=n*(K)`. Definitions 2.6-2.7 define the version space of a finite observed set and EUC: for every target and complete enumeration there is a finite prefix whose consistent-language intersection is infinite. Later prefixes preserve this property. The text recalls background results on countable/finite collections, uniform/nonuniform characterizations, EUC sufficiency, and finite unions of uniformly generatable families. These are contextual claims rather than new P10 theorems. No stochastic input distribution, training noise, loss, or sample-complexity rate is part of the main model; noisy generation is mentioned only as related work (p. 6).

**Overview claims (pp. 8-9).** Theorem 3.1: two uncountable, nonuniformly generatable collections can have a union that is not even limit-generatable. It answers both finite-union questions. Theorem 3.2: a countable nonuniformly generatable collection and an uncountable uniformly generatable collection have a nongeneratable union, with both positive generators requiring no adversary examples. The following text says making the first collection uniform, or the second countable, restores generatability, and obtains a countable union of individually uniform no-input classes that is nongeneratable. Theorem 3.3: there exists an uncountable nonuniformly generatable collection violating EUC; the following sentence specifically claims the first collection of Theorem 3.1 supplies it.

**Technical overview (pp. 10-12).** The all-infinite-subsets-of-N warm-up asserts a generator-dependent complete increasing enumeration with an error every round. The first signed attempt uses all negatives plus an infinite positive subset, and its symmetric counterpart; these are individually uniform and their union is limit-generatable by comparing longest completely observed prefixes. The modified construction allows finite omissions on one side and infinitely many omissions on the other, with the roles reversed. The displayed omission-domain constraints on p. 11 are opposite to the sides from which the sets are subtracted; the prose gives the intended signed interpretation, but that is not silently substituted here. The alternating construction claims that finite termination yields a valid hard language and infinite switching yields a valid limit language with infinitely many errors. Remark 3.4 claims both modified families are uncountable, nonuniform, and fail EUC. Remark 3.5 points to the stronger minimal-pair result.

**Detailed main claims (pp. 13-18).** Theorem 4.1 (p. 13) explicitly defines families of `(Zminus \\ A) union (Zplus \\ B)`, where A is negative and B positive: A finite/B infinite for the first; A infinite/B finite for the second. Both are trivially nonuniformly generatable; the union is not limit-generatable. Unlike the p. 11 display, the signed domains here match subtraction. Remark 4.2 (p. 15) is a separate identification assertion: the singleton family `{N}` is trivially uniformly identifiable, the countable family of finite initial segments `{1,...,i}` is trivially nonuniformly identifiable, but their union is not identifiable. These finite languages intentionally depart from the generation infinity convention. Theorem 4.3 (p. 15) defines the uncountable uniform family `Zminus union A`, A any positive subset, and the countable nonuniform family consisting of N and finite negative prefixes union a cofinite positive set. Their union defeats every deterministic generator. The source's indexing of these families is reversed relative to Theorem 3.2's countable-first presentation; this is harmless if explicitly matched. Theorem 4.4 (p. 17) uses only cofinite negative sets, asserts trivial nonuniform generation and failure of EUC, and its proof identifies the closure of a finite observation with that observation (p. 18). It does not assert uncountability; this countable family alone does not supply Theorem 3.3's uncountable witness. Its proof's final phrase says uniform generation in the empty-input modification, which raises a separate uniformity question.

**Appendix (pp. 20-22).** Proposition A.1 (p. 20): all infinite subsets of N are not deterministically limit-generatable. The proof discussion strengthens this to one sound complete increasing enumeration, chosen after G, on which every round is wrong. Proposition A.2 (pp. 20-21): for each randomized history-to-distribution generator there is one infinite K and one enumeration, depending on the generator, such that almost surely only finitely many outputs are unseen target elements. The proof uses summable `1/(n+1)^2` success bounds; independence is not stated as necessary. This is an almost-sure eventual-failure claim, stronger than failure of eventual success. Appendix A.2 describes general alternating/prefix-realizability conditions and a concrete row construction: each finite-index family has finite earlier rows, one full row, and a finite distinguished row; the infinity family has finite ordinary rows and a cofinite distinguished row, hence is trivially no-input generatable. Remark A.3 (p. 22) asserts nongeneratability of their full union and suggests grouping the infinity/odd-index families against the even-index families. The printed domain is `N x N` despite the distinguished row `-1`, and both finite-row displays index membership by `A_i` under a `k` union although the parameter list calls for `A_k`; these are source questions, not corrected definitions. Sections 1 and 5 summarize these claims; pp. 18-19 contain acknowledgments and references, with no additional formal P10 statement.

## Exact semantic basis and correspondence conventions

All ten explicit kernel types fix the alphabet to `Int`; none is polymorphic over alphabets. The ten original types and all 37 relevant definition values were rechecked in `stage2/lean-interface/declarations.jsonl`, `01-target-statements.md`, `02-definition-dependencies.md`, and `explicit-target-types/types.jsonl`. Every target is in `P/Results/Overview.lean`; its line is given in each dossier. Namespace `U` below denotes `GenLimit.UnionClosedness`, `G` denotes `GenLimit.Generic`, and `R` denotes `GenLimit.LiRamanTewari`.

Let `C` be `U.finiteNegativeInfinitePositiveClass`, `D` be `U.infiniteNegativeFinitePositiveClass`, `F` be `U.theorem43FirstClass`, and `S` be `U.theorem43SecondClass`. These are sets of sets of integers. `C,D` are exactly Theorem 4.1's two omission families (`P/MainClasses.lean:20,25,34`). In particular, the infinite set is a set of **deleted** elements, not a requirement that the retained opposite side be infinite. Deleting the entire opposite half-line is allowed. Both exclude zero. `F={Zminus union A : A subset Zplus}` (`P/MinimalPairClasses.lean:25,30`). `S` consists of `negativePrefix(i) union (Zplus minus B)`, with B finite positive, and `i=0 -> B=empty` (`:36,41,46`). Thus i=0 supplies only the separately listed N, and i>0 supplies the source's finite-prefix languages. If source N includes 0, that identification is not literal; the source's 1-based enumerations suggest positive naturals, but do not formally resolve its convention. No inserted zero is inferred.

Use `Q(g,L,x,t)` for `g t (x restricted to Fin t) in L minus sample(x,t)`, with sample the first t values (`Core/GenericGeneration.lean:37,41,63,87,367`). At time t the next stream value x(t) is not yet seen; this is the source's length-n input convention after indexing. Define:

- `I(H) = exists g, forall L in H, forall x, Injective(x) -> range(x)=L -> exists T, forall t>=T, Q(g,L,x,t)` (`P/Definitions.lean:32,41`). Hence `not I` means every generator has an infinite target, a complete injective stream, and arbitrarily late mistakes; it does not assert eventual failure or an error every round.
- `N(H) = exists g, forall L in H, exists d, forall x, range(x) subset L -> forall t, card(sample(x,t))=d -> forall s>=t, Q(g,L,x,s)` (`Core/ClassGeneration.lean:37,44`). `W(H)` moves d before L (`:26,33`). One generator serves each family; language-specific thresholds never depend on the enumeration. Streams may be incomplete or repetitive, and the threshold counts distinct samples.
- `N0(H) = exists o, Injective(o) and forall L in H, exists T_L, forall t>=T_L, o(t) in L`. `W0(H)` moves T before L (`P/WithoutAdversaryInput.lean:29,33,41,48,56`). The schedules see only time, not input history. Their outputs are globally distinct; their correctness is eventual membership, with no adversary-sample freshness predicate. Both imply that every member is infinite.
- `E(H) = forall L in H, forall x, range(x)=L -> exists t, Infinite(commonCore(H,sample(x,t)))` (`P02/EventuallyUnboundedClosure.lean:26`). The core is the intersection over members containing the sample (`Core/VersionSpace.lean:16,20`; `P02/Definitions.lean:23`). Closure here is not union of languages, a topological closure, or a sample-growth rate.

For the named infinite families, `N,W` faithfully express the source's repetition-free sample thresholds. Their sound-stream formulation is stronger for a fixed generator but equivalent at semantic existence level: finite injective prefixes extend to exhaustive enumerations; repetitive histories can be reduced to their first-occurrence histories. Conversely, distinct-sample thresholds apply directly to injective presentations. EUC on repetitive exhaustive streams is likewise equivalent to EUC on injective enumerations for infinite countable targets: every finite activating set eventually appears in either enumeration. These are elementary semantic comparisons, not claims that corresponding conversion theorems were newly checked or proved here.

The schedule predicates match the source's explicitly modified empty-input setting (p. 17 proof discussion), and strengthen it by recording nonrepetition of outputs. They must not be read as fixed history-blind outputs that remain fresh against every adversary enumeration in the original game. An adversary could enumerate each scheduled output before it is generated. The native bridge types `uniformlyGeneratable_of_withoutAdversaryInput` and `nonuniformlyGeneratable_of_withoutAdversaryInput` (`P/WithoutAdversaryInput.lean:110,134`) assert existence of ordinary fresh-output generators from schedules; they do not identify the two behaviors at every round.

All existence claims concern arbitrary total functions; neither source nor Lean types supply an effective encoding, membership oracle, computable enumeration of a countable family, uniform program taking a family as input, or runtime bound. The positive and negative classes can have different generators/schedules. No main target has a random seed, noise model, probability law, distributional loss, numerical rate, or prescribed threshold value. Class union means belonging to either collection, not pairwise union of member languages. Generated outputs need not enumerate the target, and ordinary `Q` does not require freshness relative to earlier outputs.

## Discrepancies and source questions for Stage 3

These questions are frozen before any developer explanation. They separate source inconsistencies from Lean statement scope; a difference alone is not classified as a Lean proof defect.

1. **Opposite omission domains on p. 11.** Visual inspection confirms that A is constrained positive while subtracted from negatives, and B negative while subtracted from positives. Literally those deletions do nothing, so both displayed families collapse to the nonzero integers, contradicting the asserted hardness. The p. 13 Theorem 4.1 display has the matching negative-A/positive-B domains used by Lean. The report compares the detailed theorem faithfully and flags the literal overview display as materially different, rather than rewriting the source silently.
2. **The 3.3/4.4 witness change, pp. 9,17.** The source calls 4.4 a more detailed version of the uncountable theorem, but its cofinite-negative family is countable and 4.4's displayed conclusion omits uncountability. Lean accurately distinguishes the two witnesses and provides the missing uncountable named result separately. This resolves native coverage; it does not establish that the printed derivation from 4.4 to 3.3 is justified.
3. **Uniform versus nonuniform in 4.4's proof, p. 17.** The final phrase asserts uniform generation in the empty-input modification, despite arbitrary finite deletions. A fixed globally uniform tail cannot be contained in every cofinite-negative language: remove its next scheduled element.

# Paper 16 · Language Generation: Complexity Barriers and Implications for Learning

Marcelo Arenas, Pablo Barceló, Luis Cofré, and Alexander Kozachinskiy study how many distinct positive examples a generator needs when its target belongs to a finite family of infinite languages. The source is the January 29, 2026 revision, which adds the results for locally threshold testable languages and non-erasing patterns. The arXiv metadata uses the title above. The manuscript inserts “in the Limit” after “Language Generation.” [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05759v2) · [Manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05759v2) · [Lean overview](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/Results/Overview.lean)

| Source | Lean declaration | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Theorem 3.1 | `theorem_3_1` | No partial recursive procedure can return a valid sample threshold for every pair of grammars generating infinite languages. It may diverge on inputs outside that promise. |
| Theorem 4.1(a,b) | `theorem_4_1` | A family of size $d$ over $a\ge2$ letters has threshold $a^{s^d}$ given NFAs of at most $s$ states or regexes of length at most $s-1$. For every $\ell,n\ge1$, a binary family of $d=2^\ell$ distinct infinite languages fails at $2^{n^d}$, with DFA and regex bounds $k\ell n$ and $k\ell^2n^2$ for one absolute constant $k$. |
| Theorem 5.1(a,b) | `theorem_5_1` | For a finite family $F$ of LTT languages described by finite unions of profiles of total size at most $s$ per language, set $r=s\lvert F\rvert$. The threshold is $a^{3r(r^2+1)^2}$. For every $n\ge4$, two distinct infinite single-profile languages over $n$ letters, each of profile length at most $7(n+1)$, fail at $2^n$. |
| Theorem 6.1 | `theorem_6_1` | Over a binary alphabet, two distinct infinite non-erasing pattern languages with pattern lengths exactly $44n$ and $2$ fail at $2^n$ for every positive $n$. Lean also proves that this existence statement implies $n>0$. |

## Definitions and proof correspondence

**Uniform generation.** Definition 2.2 gives the generator a finite set. At size $m$ or larger it must output a word in the target outside the observed set. Proposition 2.3 characterizes failure by a nonempty subfamily whose intersection is finite with at least $m$ words. The proof uses the library's language classes, version spaces and common cores. For classes of infinite languages, the set formulation and Core's finite-history generator have the same threshold, including on streams with repetitions. [Threshold.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/Threshold.lean) · [CoreBridge.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/CoreBridge.lean)

**3.1.** A grammar description is a start variable and a finite list of productions. Its language uses Mathlib's CFG derivability relation. Each of two grammars checks one stack of a binary Turing computation. A shared label records both stack tops and an independent bit. Disjoint private branches ensure that both grammars always generate infinite languages. A terminating trace gives a finite intersection containing all bit choices along that trace. P11's universal binary machine and recursive fixed point force an output whose number of set bits exceeds the proposed threshold. This proves the negative result for partial recursive procedures on concrete grammar descriptions. The paper uses a halting reduction via alternating reversed configurations. [Grammar.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/Grammar.lean) · [TapeGrammar.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/TapeGrammar.lean) · [ContextFreeLower.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/ContextFreeLower.lean)

**4.1.** The upper bound uses a synchronous product of NFAs and pumps a repeated state on an accepting path. A partial-derivative construction gives the required linear state bound for regular expressions. The lower bound checks how many times an address occurs between smaller addresses. Each address has one independent trailing bit. The explicit DFA consumes a complete address and then its free bit. The regular expression allows higher-address blocks before, between and after equal ones, and includes the last segment after a reset. Regex length counts the tokens of the displayed fully parenthesized grammar, with concatenation written by juxtaposition. [FiniteAutomata.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/FiniteAutomata.lean) · [RegexAutomaton.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/RegexAutomaton.lean) · [RegularLower.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/RegularLower.lean)

**5.1.** Occurrences can overlap. Thresholds are written in unary. The profile size counts the two words, seven structural tokens per profile, and five structural tokens plus the word and unary threshold per constraint. The upper proof also works with only one structural token per profile and one per comparison. It tracks the longest relevant suffix and capped occurrence counts. The lower construction splits the alphabet into two nonempty parts, with each profile limiting the counts on one part to one. Their intersection consists of all repetition-free words and contains all $n!$ permutations. [Profiles.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/Profiles.lean) · [ProfilePumping.lean](../GenLimit/Paper16_ComplexityBarriers/ProfilePumping.lean)

**6.1.** Lean uses the two length-22 blocks in the paper and the square pattern $YY$. It checks two solutions to the one-variable word equation, their independent concatenation, and finiteness of the intersection. These facts suffice for the theorem's lower bound. The source says “for every $n\in\mathbb N$.” With Lean's zero-inclusive naturals, the empty pattern at $n=0$ generates only the empty word. The overview gives the exact range of the theorem.
